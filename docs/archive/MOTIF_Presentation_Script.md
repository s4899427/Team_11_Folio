## Slide 2 — Domain & Problem Space

So what's the actual problem we're solving?

Globally, people spend almost 21 hours a week listening to music, and 44% are actively discovering new artists weekly — that's IFPI's 2023 global survey data.

But recent research from this year's WWW conference found something interesting: recommendation algorithms actually narrow what we're exposed to rather than opening it up — they flatten the differences between what different people hear.

And this isn't just one of us saying it — three of us interviewed completely different people, independently, and got the same answer back. Dylan's participant Aloysious said, quoting directly, *"I just want it to be more human focused. Algorithm has ruined the taste — no fun, no randomness to it."* Pranav's participant said, *"Just tell me what's happening near me that people I trust are into. Not what a machine thinks I'll click."* And Mohammad's participant Minahil, a resident doctor, said everything feels very algorithm-driven and she wants to discover things more naturally.

Three separate interviews, same gap. That's what we're designing into.

---

## Slide 3 — Design Opportunity & Concept

So here's our opportunity. We're building a living, wiki-style space for world culture — pick any tradition and see it holistically. Not just the music, but the instruments, the fashion and fabric, the food, the art, the stories, tied together in one place instead of scattered across five different searches.

Streaming music and a lightweight remix feature called Drop sit alongside this, but the encyclopedic core is really the point.

Just to be clear — these culture examples on screen (Aboriginal Australian art, Japanese street fashion, West African highlife, Andean weaving, K-pop culture) are illustrative only. The framework's meant to generalise across any culture, not just these five.

We looked at what's already out there: Spotify and Apple Music are solo listening with social as an afterthought. Wikipedia is deep on facts but has zero lived culture, no music, no community voice. Instagram and TikTok are where discovery actually happens today, but it's ephemeral and unsearchable. And artist-follow apps like Bandsintown are useless if you're exploring a culture you haven't met yet.

---

## Slide 4 — Audience

None of this is guesswork — it's grounded in six real interviews we've already conducted, across a real spread of ages, professions and backgrounds: a resident doctor, two university students, two ex-UQ graduates, and one international participant based in India.

A few patterns came through clearly.

On **values**: people want authorship over their own taste, and credit going to the person who put them onto something — not the platform.

On **workflows**: it's screenshot, group chat, forget — discovery happens socially and then just dies in the camera roll.

On **skills**: our participants are already fluent across four different apps just to express one identity, and none of those tools treat curation as real work.

And on **priorities**: people want low effort on ordinary days, but they're willing to put in real effort for an occasion — and several told us local relevance beats broad browsing every time.

---

## Slide 5 — Plan of Work

So what's left to figure out?

We've still got open questions: how much of the app should be encyclopedic wiki content versus the social Drop and Remix feature; whether people will actually contribute cultural content or just consume it; how we represent culture respectfully — especially something like Indigenous traditions, which we think has to be community-authored rather than written top-down by us; and whether local relevance really does beat broad discovery, which is what our interviews are pointing to so far.

Our methods going forward: we've done six semi-structured interviews, with about two more planned, plus a one-week cultural probe diary study, contextual observation at a local venue and market, a paper-prototype comparison test, and think-aloud usability testing once we start building.

Timeline-wise: Week 6 we finish interviews and get concept variants onto paper. Week 7 we test with five participants and lock the concept. Weeks 8 and 9, information architecture and a clickable mid-fidelity Figma prototype. Weeks 10 and 11, we build the real core loop, simulating everything around it. Weeks 12 and 13, usability testing and final documentation.

Thanks — happy to take any questions.
